1 What do you think? Choose True, False, or It depends for each statement.
1
Reading directly from your slides is a perfectly acceptable presentation technique.
False — slides should support you, not be your script. Reading from them breaks eye contact, loses audience engagement, and makes you seem unprepared. Your slides are visual cues; your voice delivers the message.
2
The opening of a presentation is the most important part.
True — first impressions set the tone. A strong hook captures attention immediately and frames everything that follows. Audiences decide very quickly whether a presenter is worth listening to, so the opening must engage them right away.
3
Using lots of complex data in your slides makes you look more professional.
False — clarity beats complexity. Too much data overwhelms audiences and can obscure your main message. Effective presenters select the most relevant data, present it visually, and explain its significance in simple terms.
4
It's better to have fewer slides with more content on each one.
False — less is more. Dense, text-heavy slides tire audiences quickly. It is better to use clean, visual slides with minimal text and let your spoken words do the work. The golden rule: one key idea per slide.

How to Structure a Presentation That Gets Results

Many presenters focus so much on their content that they forget about structure — and this is where most presentations fail. No matter how good your ideas are, if they are not organised clearly, your audience will struggle to follow, and your message will be lost. The most reliable framework for professional presentations is the classic Tell-Tell-Tell structure: tell them what you're going to say, say it, then tell them what you said. This three-part approach — introduction, body, conclusion — ensures your audience always knows where they are in the presentation.

The introduction is your most important moment. This is where you grab attention with a hook — a surprising statistic, a bold question, a short story, or a striking image. After the hook, give your audience a clear outline: let them know exactly what you will cover and in what order. Finishing with a brief statement of what they will gain by the end — "By the end of today, you'll have a clear action plan" — gives them a reason to stay engaged.

The body of your presentation is where signposting language becomes essential. Signposting phrases — such as "Moving on to...", "This brings me to...", or "As I mentioned earlier..." — are the verbal signs that guide your audience from one section to the next. Without them, even a well-researched presentation can feel disjointed and hard to follow. Good transitions are the invisible glue that holds a presentation together.

A strong conclusion is more than a summary — it is your last chance to make an impression. Effective closings include a summary of the key points, a memorable quote, a call to action, or an invitation for questions. Research shows that audiences remember the first and last things they hear most clearly. So if your opening hook grabs attention, your closing statement must leave a lasting impression. Never end a presentation with "That's it, really" — end with intention and impact.

2 Choose the best signposting phrase for each situation.
1. You want to move from your introduction to your first main point.
✅ "Moving on to our first topic..." — this is a classic transition phrase that signals to the audience you are leaving the introduction and entering the body. Option A is for concluding; Option C is for linking back to something already said.
2. You want to add a supporting point to something you've just said.
✅ "Furthermore, the data shows..." — "furthermore" (and alternatives like "what's more" or "in addition") signals that you are adding information to support the same point. Option B moves to a new section; Option C closes the presentation.
3. You want to refer back to something you said at the beginning of the presentation.
✅ "As I mentioned at the beginning..." — this phrase explicitly links back to an earlier point, creating cohesion in the presentation. Option A moves forward; Option B introduces an example.
4. You are wrapping up the presentation and want to summarise.
✅ "To summarise the key points..." — this is a classic closing signal that tells the audience you are moving into the conclusion. Option B is for opening; Option C is for adding information, not closing.

Speaking
3 Challenges
Situation 1 of 3
Challenge 1 · 90-Second Opening
Deliver just the opening of a presentation on this topic: "Why our team should switch to remote-first working." Include a hook, tell the audience what you'll cover, and introduce your first point.
💡 Use: "I'd like to start with a question..." / "Today I'm going to cover three areas..." / "By the end of this, you'll understand why..."
"Good morning, everyone. I'd like to start with a question: how many of you spent more than two hours in commuting this week? [pause] According to a recent survey, the average UK employee loses 220 hours a year to commuting. That's over nine full days. Today I'm going to talk about why switching to a remote-first model could be one of the best decisions our team makes this year. I'll cover three areas: the impact on productivity, the benefits for recruitment, and the cost savings for the business. By the end of this, I hope you'll agree that it's time to rethink how we work. Let me start with productivity..."
Challenge 2 · Transition Practice
You've just finished explaining the problem (staff turnover is increasing). Now transition smoothly into your proposed solution using signposting language. The audience should clearly feel the shift from problem to solution.
💡 Use: "Now that we've looked at the problem..." / "This brings me to..." / "So what's the solution? Let me turn to..."
"So, as we've seen, staff turnover has risen by 18% over the past two years, and the cost of replacing each employee is significant. Now that we've looked at the scale of the problem, this brings me to the second part of my presentation: the solution. I'd like to propose a new employee retention programme that focuses on three key areas. Let's turn to that now..."
Challenge 3 · Closing with Impact
You are ending a presentation on company results. Summarise three key points from your presentation, give a clear call to action, and invite questions from the audience.
💡 Use: "To summarise the key points..." / "The one thing I'd like you to take away is..." / "I'll now open the floor for questions."
"To summarise the key points from today: first, Q3 revenue exceeded our target by 12%. Second, our new product line has outperformed all projections in the first month. And third, we have successfully expanded into two new markets. The one thing I'd like you to take away from today is this: our strategy is working, and the momentum is with us. Going into Q4, I'd encourage every team to focus on maintaining that pace. Thank you very much for your time and attention. I'll now open the floor for questions — please feel free to ask about anything we've covered today."
💡 Useful signposting phrases
"I'd like to start by giving you some background..." — opening
"This brings me to my next point..." — transitioning
"Let me give you an example..." — illustrating
"To sum up what we've covered today..." — summarising
"I'll now open the floor for questions." — inviting Q&A
