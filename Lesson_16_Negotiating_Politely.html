Warm-up — "Negotiation Instincts"
True / False / It depends
💡 Last lesson you learned to back up an argument with facts and figures. Now let's put that data to work — in a negotiation.
1 What do you think? Choose True, False, or It depends for each statement.
1
The goal of a good negotiation is to get as much as possible for yourself.
False — the best negotiations create value for both sides. Squeezing every last point out of the other party often damages the relationship and makes future deals harder. Skilled negotiators look for outcomes where both sides feel they've won something.
2
Staying silent for a few seconds after an offer can be a powerful negotiating tool.
True — silence creates pressure. Many people feel uncomfortable with pauses and rush to fill them, sometimes by improving their offer. Confident negotiators let silence do the work instead of talking themselves into a worse deal.
3
You should always be the first person to name a number or price.
It depends — going first lets you "anchor" the discussion around your number, which can work in your favour. But if you don't know the market well, it can be safer to let the other side speak first so you don't underestimate your position.
4
A compromise means both sides walk away equally unhappy.
False — a good compromise isn't about splitting the difference and disappointing everyone. It's about finding creative trade-offs so each side gives up something they value less in exchange for something they value more.

Negotiating Without Burning Bridges

Many people picture negotiation as a battle: one side wins, the other loses. In business, this mindset rarely serves you well, because most negotiations happen between people who will keep working together — a supplier and a buyer, a manager and a team, two departments sharing a budget. The most effective negotiators focus on interests, not positions. A position is what someone says they want ("We need a 15% discount"); an interest is why they want it ("We're under pressure to reduce costs this quarter"). Understanding the interest behind a position often reveals creative solutions — perhaps a longer contract, faster payment terms, or added services could solve the real problem without touching the price at all.

Before entering any negotiation, experienced professionals prepare their BATNA — their Best Alternative To a Negotiated Agreement. In plain terms: what will you do if this deal falls through? Knowing your BATNA gives you confidence and a clear walk-away point, so you're never forced to accept a bad deal out of fear or pressure.

Throughout the discussion, tone matters as much as content. Rather than making blunt demands, skilled negotiators use polite, indirect language: "Would it be possible to..." instead of "You need to...", or "We'd be in a much stronger position if..." instead of "That's not good enough." This softer language keeps the conversation collaborative rather than confrontational, even when the two sides disagree.

Finally, every concession should be a trade, not a gift. If you lower your price, ask for something in return — a longer contract, an upfront payment, a public reference. This principle, sometimes summarised as "never give something for nothing," protects your position and signals that every point in the deal has real value.

Grammar
Softening & Polite Negotiation Language
1 Study the key polite negotiation structures, then do the practice exercise below.
🔷 Softening a request (modals)
would "Would it be possible to extend the deadline by a week?"
could "Could we perhaps look at a slightly lower minimum order?"
might "We might be able to work with that, if..."
🔶 Conditionals for hypothetical offers
1st cond. "If you can commit to a 12-month contract, we'll reduce the price by 5%."
2nd cond. "If we lowered the price, would you be able to pay upfront?"
mixed "We'd be in a stronger position if we'd known about this earlier."
🔸 Making & requesting concessions
offering "We'd be willing to..., provided that..."
requesting "In return, we'd need you to..."
testing "What if we met in the middle on...?"
🔹 Disagreeing politely
pushback "I understand your position, but that would be difficult for us."
reframing "We'd be in a much stronger position if we approached this differently."
closing "I think we're very close — let's find a way to bridge that gap."
2 Choose the most appropriately polite way to say each thing.
1. You want to ask for a lower minimum order quantity.
✅ "Would it be possible to..." — this modal structure softens the request without weakening your position. Option A is a direct demand; Option C is a flat refusal — both risk sounding confrontational.
2. You want to offer a discount, but only if the client commits to a longer contract.
✅ The first conditional clearly links the condition (12-month contract) to the reward (5% reduction) — this is a classic trade-off structure. Options A and C sound careless or unprofessional.
3. You disagree with a proposal but want to keep the discussion friendly.
✅ "I understand your position, but..." — acknowledging the other side's view before disagreeing keeps the tone collaborative. Options A and C are blunt and can escalate tension unnecessarily.
4. You want to suggest a compromise between two positions.
✅ "What if we met in the middle on...?" — this phrase gently tests a compromise without committing either side. Options B and C shut down the conversation instead of moving it forward.

Part 5
Speaking
3 Challenges
Situation 1 of 3
Challenge 1 · Opening a Negotiation with Data
A supplier wants to raise their prices by 10%. Using the trend language from last lesson, open the negotiation by presenting evidence and stating your position politely — without immediately rejecting the offer.
💡 Use: "The data suggests..." / "Given that our order volume has increased by..." / "We'd be in a stronger position if..."
"Thanks for sending this over. Before we agree to anything, I'd like to share some context. Our order volume with you has risen steadily by around 18% over the past year, and it's projected to keep growing. Given that, a 10% price increase feels like it's moving in the opposite direction to where our relationship is heading. Would it be possible to look at a smaller increase, perhaps in exchange for a longer-term commitment on our side?"
Challenge 2 · Making a Concession
You are willing to accept a 6% increase, but only if the supplier agrees to guarantee delivery within 48 hours. Make this trade-off offer clearly and politely, using conditional language.
💡 Use: "We'd be willing to..., provided that..." / "In return, we'd need..." / "If you could..., we would..."
"Okay — let me propose something. We'd be willing to accept a 6% increase, provided that you could guarantee delivery within 48 hours on every order, not just priority ones. If you could commit to that in writing, we would be happy to sign a renewed 12-month agreement today. Does that work from your side?"
Challenge 3 · Breaking a Deadlock
The two sides have reached a deadlock: you won't go above 4%, they won't go below 8%. Suggest a creative compromise that moves the conversation forward.
💡 Use: "I think we're very close..." / "What if we met in the middle on...?" / "Could we perhaps..."
"I think we're very close here, and I don't want a couple of percentage points to get in the way of a good partnership. What if we met in the middle at 6%, but locked that rate in for two years instead of one? That gives you price certainty over a longer period, and it protects us from further increases in the meantime. Could we perhaps agree on that basis?"
💡 Useful negotiation phrases
"Would it be possible to...?" — softening a request
"We'd be willing to..., provided that..." — offering a conditional concession
"What if we met in the middle on...?" — proposing a compromise
"I understand your position, but..." — disagreeing politely
"I think we're very close — let's bridge that gap." — closing a deal

Speed Negotiation
1 Speed Negotiation — 3 minutes to prepare, 4 minutes to negotiate.
🤝 Your Challenge

Work in pairs. One person is the buyer, one is the seller, negotiating the price and terms for office furniture for a new branch. You each have secret instructions — don't show them to your partner!

Buyer's brief: Budget of €18,000. Ideally want 60-day payment terms. Walk-away point: €22,000.
Seller's brief: List price €25,000. Can go as low as €19,000 if paid within 15 days. Walk-away point: €19,000.
Feedback focus: Did both sides use polite, indirect language? Was every concession traded for something in return? Did they find a deal, or reach deadlock? Report back to the group on what you agreed.
One possible deal: €20,500, paid within 30 days, with the seller including free delivery and setup. Neither side reached their walk-away point, and both gained something they valued: the buyer got acceptable payment terms, and the seller secured a faster payment than their original 60-day fear.

Tip: The best outcomes usually involve trading something beyond price alone — payment terms, delivery, contract length, or added services.
