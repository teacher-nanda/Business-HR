Part 1
Warm-up
True / False / It depends

You've just interviewed someone. Now imagine they've been working with you for 6 months — it's time for their performance review.

1 What do you think? Choose True, False, or It depends for each statement.
1
Feedback should always be positive — negative comments damage motivation.
False — Constructive feedback, when delivered well, drives growth. The delivery matters, not avoiding it. Avoiding negative feedback entirely can prevent people from improving and succeeding.
2
You should only give feedback during formal reviews, not throughout the year.
False — Ongoing feedback is more effective than saving everything for one annual review. Regular check-ins allow people to adjust behaviour before issues become serious problems.
3
It's better to give feedback in writing so the employee has time to process it.
It depends — on the type of feedback. Serious feedback needs a face-to-face conversation where tone and body language help deliver the message sensitively. Minor praise, however, can work well in writing.
4
A good manager should tell employees what to improve AND what they are doing well.
True — Balanced feedback acknowledges strengths and identifies areas for growth. This is the foundation of an effective performance review. People are more receptive to constructive criticism when they also hear what they do well.

The SBI Model: Giving Feedback That Actually Works

Giving feedback is one of the most important — and most difficult — skills a manager can develop. Without a clear structure, feedback often becomes vague ("You need to communicate better"), too personal ("You always do this"), or so softened that the message is lost completely. The SBI model offers a simple, professional framework that keeps feedback specific, objective, and constructive.

SBI stands for Situation, Behaviour, and Impact. The first step is to describe the Situation — the specific context in which the behaviour occurred. This gives the feedback a clear anchor and prevents it from feeling like a general criticism. For example: "In yesterday's team meeting..." Describing the situation first sets the scene without making the employee feel attacked.

Next comes Behaviour — what the person actually did, described in objective, observable terms. This is the most critical part of the model. Instead of saying "you were rude," a manager using SBI would say "you interrupted three colleagues before they had finished speaking." The focus is on the action, not the personality. Finally, Impact describes the effect of that behaviour: "The client felt the meeting was disorganised and mentioned it in their follow-up email." Linking behaviour to real impact makes the feedback meaningful and motivating.

After delivering SBI feedback, effective managers don't just stop there. They invite the employee into the conversation by asking questions such as "What do you think you could do differently?" or "How would you approach this next time?" This transforms feedback from a one-way assessment into a two-way dialogue, which leads to stronger commitment and better outcomes.

Part 4
Grammar
Modal Verbs for Advice & Suggestions
1 Study the key language, then do the practice exercise below.
🔷 Strong advice — should / ought to
should You should prepare specific examples before the review.
ought to She ought to address the communication issue directly.
➜ Use should and ought to when you believe something is clearly the right course of action.
🔶 Softer suggestions — could / might want to / it might be worth
could You could try scheduling weekly check-ins.
might want to You might want to speak with HR about this.
it might be worth It might be worth asking for feedback more regularly.
➜ Use these to make gentle, non-imposing suggestions. They leave the decision with the other person.
🔸 Negative advice — shouldn't / would advise against
shouldn't You shouldn't wait until the annual review to raise concerns.
would advise against I would advise against sending that email without checking first.
➜ Use these to clearly discourage an action, with varying degrees of formality.
2 Choose the correct option to complete each sentence.
1. "I think you _____ speak to HR about this situation — it sounds serious."
✅ should — the phrase "it sounds serious" signals that this is strong, clear advice, not a gentle suggestion. Should is the most direct modal here.
2. "It _____ be worth scheduling a follow-up meeting next week to check in."
✅ might — the fixed phrase is "it might be worth + -ing". This is a soft suggestion, leaving the choice with the other person. It should be worth is not a natural expression.
3. "You _____ send that email without checking with your manager first."
✅ shouldn't — this is negative advice: you are saying it would be wrong or unwise to do something. Couldn't talks about ability, and wouldn't is about willingness — neither fits advice here.
4. "If you're not sure how to approach it, you _____ ask a colleague for advice."
✅ could — the "if you're not sure" opener signals that a soft, optional suggestion is appropriate. Should would be too strong, and must would be too forceful for this context.

Part 5
Speaking
3 Scenarios
Situation 1 of 3
Situation 1 · Delivering Positive Feedback
A team member just led a client presentation brilliantly. It was their first time leading one independently, and the client was very impressed. Give them specific, meaningful praise using the SBI model.
💡 Use: "I wanted to give you some feedback on..." / "Specifically, when you... I noticed that..." / "The impact of this was..."
"I wanted to give you some feedback on yesterday's client presentation — you did a brilliant job. Specifically, when the client asked that difficult question about pricing, you stayed completely calm and gave a really clear, confident answer. I noticed you had prepared for that scenario in advance. The impact of this was significant — the client said it was one of the most professional meetings they'd had, and they've already asked for a follow-up. Well done."
Situation 2 · Constructive Feedback
A colleague regularly arrives 10–15 minutes late to team meetings. It's becoming disruptive. Address this professionally using the SBI model — without damaging the working relationship.
💡 Use: "I wanted to have a quick word about..." / "I've noticed that recently..." / "Going forward, it might be worth..."
"I wanted to have a quick word — I hope that's okay. I've noticed that over the past few weeks, you've been arriving to our Monday morning meetings around 10 to 15 minutes late. The impact of this is that we end up repeating information for you, which takes extra time, and it can sometimes affect the team's focus. I'm not sure if something is making it difficult for you — is everything okay? Going forward, it might be worth letting me know in advance if you're going to be late. Would that work?"
Situation 3 · Performance Review Conversation
You are reviewing an employee who has improved significantly over the past six months but still has one clear area to develop: meeting deadlines on reports. Balance the positive feedback and the development area, then agree on a goal together.
💡 Use: "Overall, I'm really pleased with..." / "One area I'd like us to focus on is..." / "What do you think you could do differently?"
"Overall, I'm really pleased with how much you've developed this year. Your client relationships have been excellent — three clients specifically mentioned you by name in their feedback, which is fantastic. The one area I'd like us to focus on going forward is report deadlines. In the past quarter, two of your monthly reports were submitted late, and this created some knock-on effects for the finance team. I'm not looking to criticise — I want us to find a solution together. What do you think gets in the way? And what could we put in place to help you hit those deadlines consistently this quarter?"
💡 Useful phrases
"I wanted to give you some feedback on..." — opening the conversation
"Specifically, when you... I noticed that..." — SBI: Situation + Behaviour
"The impact of this was..." — SBI: Impact
"Going forward, it might be worth..." — making a constructive suggestion
"What do you think you could do differently?" — inviting the employee's input

👤 Employee Profile Card
Name: Alex
Role: Sales Executive
Strength: Client relationships
Area to improve: Reporting deadlines
Agreed goal: Submit all monthly reports on time this quarter
Instructions: Student A = Manager · Student B = Alex (the employee). The manager should: open the review, give positive SBI feedback on client relationships, introduce the development area, and agree on the goal together. The employee can ask questions and contribute ideas.
Manager: Thanks for coming in, Alex. How are you feeling about the review today?
Alex: A little nervous, to be honest! But ready.
Manager: There's nothing to worry about — you've had a really strong six months. Let me start with something specific. In last month's client meeting with Hartmann & Co., you handled a very difficult negotiation really professionally. You stayed calm, found a middle ground, and the client renewed their contract. The impact was huge — that account is now worth 20% more than last year.
Alex: Thank you — that one was quite stressful at the time!
Manager: You didn't show it. Now, there's one area I'd like us to focus on going forward. The monthly sales reports — in Q2, two of them came in after the deadline. I know it's not intentional, but it creates knock-on issues for the finance team. What do you think gets in the way?
Alex: Honestly, I tend to leave them until the last day. I think if I blocked time in my calendar earlier in the month, that would help.
Manager: That sounds like a great plan. Let's set a goal: all reports submitted on time this quarter. Does that feel achievable?
Alex: Absolutely. I'll block Thursday mornings for it.
