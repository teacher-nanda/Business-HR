Warm-up — "Data Detectives"
True / False / It depends
💡 In your last pitch you backed up your claims with data. Now let's get comfortable reading and talking about numbers.
1 What do you think? Choose True, False, or It depends for each statement.
1
In business, using percentages is always more convincing than using raw numbers.
It depends — Both can be powerful. "80% of customers" is memorable and relatable; "1.2 million users" can be more impressive when you want to show scale. Choose based on your audience and context.
2
If a trend has been going up for three months, it will definitely continue to go up.
False — Trends can reverse at any time. Business context and market forces matter. Never assume a trend will continue without analysis and supporting evidence.
3
It's always better to present exact figures rather than approximate ones.
It depends — Exact numbers add credibility for key statistics. But approximate figures ("roughly a third", "approximately 500") are often better for general points — they're easier to absorb and sound less like you're over-claiming.
4
A sharp rise in sales is always good news for a company.
False — Not necessarily. A sudden spike could indicate a one-off event, seasonal effect, or accounting change. It could also mask other problems. Context is everything when interpreting data.

Talking Numbers: The Language of Business Trends

In business presentations, numbers rarely speak for themselves. The language you use to describe them is just as important as the figures. A rise can be described as a "surge," an "increase," or an "uptick" — each word creates a different impression of scale and speed. Similarly, a fall can "plummet," "decline," or "level off." Choosing the right verb, combined with an adverb such as "sharply," "steadily," or "gradually," gives your audience a clear and vivid picture of what the data shows.

When presenting data, the passive voice is often the professional's choice. Rather than saying "we increased sales," the formal report style prefers "sales were reported to have increased" or "it has been found that." This impersonal style removes personal responsibility and sounds more objective — which is especially important when the news is mixed or the source of the data needs to be acknowledged.

Good communicators also know how to hedge — that is, to express appropriate caution about a claim. Phrases such as "it appears that," "the data suggests," and "this should be interpreted with caution" signal to the audience that you are being careful, not over-claiming. Hedging is not a sign of weakness; it is a sign of credibility.

Finally, the most important skill when presenting trends is contextualisation. A figure on its own — "revenue rose by 12%" — tells only half the story. The skilled presenter goes further: "Revenue rose by 12%, which is particularly significant given that the overall market contracted by 3% over the same period." This is what turns raw data into a compelling business argument.

Grammar
Passive Voice for Reporting Data
1 Study the key language, then do the practice exercise below.
🔷 Active → Passive for impersonal reporting
active The team increased sales by 20%.
➜ Passive: Sales were increased by 20%. (impersonal, formal)
active Researchers found that demand was rising.
➜ Passive: It was found that demand was rising.
🔶 Common passive reporting structures
present It is estimated that the market will grow by 10%.
present Revenue is expected to reach €2M by year end.
past It has been reported that sales rose sharply in Q3.
➜ These structures sound objective and formal — ideal for business presentations and reports.
🔸 Verbs + adverbs for describing trends
rise / fall + sharply · steadily · gradually · significantly · slightly · dramatically
➜ Sales rose sharply. · Costs have fallen steadily. · Profits declined slightly.
2 Choose the correct option to complete each sentence.
1. Sales ___ by 15% last quarter, according to the annual report.
✅ were reported to have increased — this is the formal passive reporting structure used in business reports. Option A is active voice; option C is present perfect but lacks the passive reporting structure.
2. It ___ that the market will grow by 12% next year.
✅ is expected — the passive structure "It is expected that..." is used for forecasts and projections in formal English. "Expects" is active (who is expecting?). "Is expecting" is continuous and does not fit.
3. Profits ___ since the new strategy was introduced six months ago.
✅ have risen steadily — the present perfect is used for a trend that started in the past and continues to now ("since the strategy was introduced"). "Rose" is simple past (finished action). "Are risen" is not a correct form in English.
4. The number of remote workers ___ to double by 2027, according to industry analysts.
✅ is projected — "is projected to" is a passive structure used for formal forecasts. "Projects" is active (the number doesn't project anything itself). "Is projecting" is active continuous and does not fit this context.

Speaking
3 Data Scenarios
Situation 1 of 3
Situation 1 · Describing a bar chart
Describe a bar chart showing quarterly revenue over one year: Q1 rose by 5%, Q2 saw a sharp rise of 18%, Q3 levelled off, and Q4 showed a slight decline. Use trend language and at least two adverbs.
💡 Use: "In the first quarter..." / "This was followed by a sharp rise..." / "By Q4, revenue had declined slightly..."
"Looking at this bar chart, we can see that revenue rose gradually in Q1 — a modest increase of around 5%. This was followed by a significant surge in Q2, where revenue climbed sharply by 18% — by far the strongest quarter of the year. In Q3, growth levelled off and remained largely stable. Then in Q4, we saw a slight decline, which it is worth noting coincided with our competitor's product launch. Overall, the year showed strong performance, though Q4 warrants further analysis."
Situation 2 · Adding context with hedging
A colleague says: "Sales doubled last quarter — that's amazing!" You need to add important context: the growth happened because of a one-time promotional event. Respond using hedging language to avoid over-claiming.
💡 Use: "It's worth noting that..." / "However, this should be interpreted with caution..." / "The data suggests..."
"That's great news — and the figures are genuinely impressive. However, I think this should be interpreted with some caution. The data suggests that the doubling in sales is largely attributable to the flash promotion we ran in week two. It appears that a significant portion of those purchases were one-off buyers, not repeat customers. It is worth noting that our week-on-week retention rate actually remained flat. So while the headline figure is positive, the underlying trend is less clear. Let's look at the next quarter before drawing any firm conclusions."
Situation 3 · Making data interesting
Present three key statistics from your industry (real or invented). For each one, use trend language, a passive reporting structure, or hedging — and explain what the statistic means for your work. Make the data come alive.
💡 Use: "It is estimated that..." / "Year-on-year, this represents..." / "What this means for us is..."
"First: it is estimated that global e-commerce sales will reach €6 trillion by 2026 — year-on-year growth of approximately 10%. For our team, this means the shift to digital is not optional; it is essential.

Second: it has been reported that 73% of consumers now expect a personalised experience when they shop online. This suggests our current generic approach is losing us customers we could retain.

Third: average cart abandonment rates have surged to 70% across our sector. The data suggests that better checkout design alone could recover a significant portion of lost revenue."
💡 Useful data language phrases
"The figures show a steady increase in..." — describing a trend
"This represents a rise of approximately..." — giving approximate numbers
"It is worth noting that..." — drawing attention to important context
"However, this should be interpreted with caution because..." — hedging
"Year-on-year, the data suggests..." — comparing over time

📊 Data Story

Find or invent three statistics about your company or industry. Present them to the class in 90 seconds. You must use: trend language (at least two verbs + adverbs), the passive voice at least once, and at least one hedging phrase.

After your presentation, your audience asks you one follow-up question. Answer it using data language.

Think about: what story does the data tell? · what context does the audience need? · what conclusion are you leading them towards?

Stat 1: "It is estimated that 40% of our target customers now research products exclusively on mobile devices. Year-on-year, that figure has risen steadily — up from 28% just two years ago."

Stat 2: "Average page load times in our sector have been reported to have declined to under two seconds. However, this should be interpreted with caution — our own site currently takes four seconds, which may be costing us customers."

Stat 3: "Customer loyalty scores have fluctuated throughout the year, though it appears that Q3 — when we launched our new support system — saw the strongest performance."

Follow-up Q: "Where did these figures come from?"
"Great question. Statistics 1 and 3 come from our internal CRM data. Statistic 2 is from an industry benchmarking report published last quarter — I'll share the link after."
