Part 1
Warm-up
True / False / It depends
1 Have You Ever? Read each statement and choose True, False, or It depends.
1
Reading a job description carefully before an interview is unnecessary — you should just be yourself.
False — Knowing the job description inside out helps you tailor your answers and show the interviewer you are the right fit. Being yourself is important, but preparation is what makes you stand out.
2
It is better to give long, detailed answers to every interview question, no matter what is asked.
It depends — Behavioral questions (e.g. "Tell me about a time when...") benefit from detailed, structured answers. But simple questions like "Where are you based?" need concise replies. Reading the room and the question matters enormously.
3
Nervousness can actually help you perform better in an interview.
True — A little adrenaline sharpens your focus and signals that you care about the outcome. The key is channelling it productively rather than trying to hide it. Many experienced candidates use their nerves as energy.
4
Interviewers decide if they like a candidate within the first few minutes of meeting them.
True — Research shows that first impressions form very quickly, often within the first 30–90 seconds. Body language, your greeting, and your overall presence all play a significant role before you have even answered a single question.

The STAR Method: Your Secret Interview Weapon

Job interviews can be unpredictable, but one type of question appears in almost every professional interview: the behavioral question. These questions typically begin with phrases such as "Tell me about a time when..." or "Can you give me an example of...". Interviewers use them because research shows that past behaviour is one of the best predictors of future performance. In other words, how you handled a challenge last year tells them a great deal about how you will handle challenges in their organisation.

To answer behavioral questions effectively, many candidates use the STAR method — a simple four-part structure. S stands for Situation: set the scene briefly and give the context. T stands for Task: explain what your specific responsibility or goal was. A stands for Action: describe what steps you personally took to address the situation. Finally, R stands for Result: explain what happened as a consequence of your actions. The Result part should be as specific as possible — ideally including measurable outcomes such as percentages, time saved, or revenue generated.

Many candidates make the mistake of spending too long on the Situation and not enough time on the Action and Result. Interviewers are most interested in what you did and what the impact was. Try to keep your Situation and Task brief — no more than two or three sentences — and devote the majority of your answer to the Action and Result stages.

Here is a worked example. Question: "Tell me about a time when you managed a project under pressure." Situation: "Our lead developer left unexpectedly two weeks before a major product launch." Task: "As project manager, I was responsible for ensuring the launch went ahead on time." Action: "I immediately redistributed tasks, brought in a freelance developer, and ran daily check-ins to keep the team on track." Result: "The team delivered the project on time, the launch was a success, and the client was so pleased that they renewed their contract for another two years."

Grammar
Relative Clauses
1 Study the key language, then complete the practice exercise below.
🔷 Defining Relative Clauses — who / that / which
people I managed a team who worked across three time zones.
➜ Use who or that for people in defining clauses.
things I worked on a project that reduced costs by 20%.
➜ Use that or which for things in defining clauses.
time Can you describe a moment when you showed leadership?
🔶 Relative Clause in the Middle
example The strategy that I developed was adopted company-wide.
➜ The relative clause can interrupt the main clause — keep normal word order inside it.
🔸 Non-Defining Relative Clauses (extra information, use commas)
example My manager, who has 20 years of experience, mentored me throughout.
➜ Commas signal extra (non-essential) information. Use which, NOT that, for things in non-defining clauses.
2 Choose the correct relative pronoun to complete each sentence.
1. "I led a team ___ achieved record sales last quarter."
✅ who — the relative clause refers to "a team" (people). Who or that are both correct for people, but who is the most natural choice here.
2. "The project ___ I managed won an industry award."
✅ that — the relative clause refers to "the project" (a thing). That or which are correct. Note: what is never used as a relative pronoun in this way.
3. "Can you describe a time ___ you dealt with a difficult client?"
✅ when — the relative clause modifies "a time" (a point in time). We use when to refer to a time or moment.
4. "My previous role, ___ I held for three years, gave me excellent leadership experience."
✅ which — this is a non-defining relative clause (notice the commas). For things in non-defining clauses, we use which, NOT that.

Part 5
Speaking
3 Interview Role-plays
Situation 1 of 3
Situation 1 · "Tell me about yourself"
Give a 2-minute professional pitch in response to the classic opening question: "So, tell me a little bit about yourself." Remember: keep it professional, structured, and relevant to the role. This is not a life story!
💡 Structure: current role → key experience → why you are here. Aim for 4–6 sentences.
"Of course — I'm currently a Senior Account Manager at a financial services company, where I've been for the past four years. Before that, I spent three years in business development at a technology start-up, which is where I really developed my client-facing skills. Throughout my career, I've focused on building long-term client relationships and driving revenue growth — in fact, I led a team that achieved a 35% increase in annual sales last year. I'm now looking to take on a broader leadership role, and when I saw this position, I felt it was a perfect fit for both my experience and my ambitions. I'm really excited about the opportunity."
Situation 2 · Behavioral Question
The interviewer asks: "Tell me about a time when you had to deal with a difficult colleague. How did you handle it?" Use the STAR method (Situation → Task → Action → Result) to give a structured, professional answer.
💡 Keep Situation and Task brief. Spend most time on Action and Result. Be honest but diplomatic.
Situation: "A few years ago, I was working on a cross-functional project with a colleague who had a very different working style to mine — they often missed deadlines and were resistant to feedback."

Task: "As the project lead, it was my responsibility to ensure we delivered on time, so I needed to address the issue directly but constructively."

Action: "I requested a one-to-one meeting and approached it with curiosity rather than criticism — I asked them what was making the project difficult for them. It turned out they were overloaded with other work. We agreed to reprioritise tasks together, and I gave them clearer deadlines with earlier check-in points."

Result: "The project was delivered on schedule. More importantly, our working relationship improved significantly, and we actually collaborated again on a later project very successfully."
Situation 3 · "Where do you see yourself in five years?"
Answer the question: "Where do you see yourself in five years?" Be ambitious but realistic. Show career drive while connecting your answer to what this company or role can offer you.
💡 Avoid: "I want your job!" or "I have no idea." Connect your goals to the company's growth or values.
"In five years, I would love to have grown into a senior leadership role — ideally managing a team and contributing to strategic decisions at a higher level. I'm genuinely passionate about this industry, and I see this company as a place where I could build that kind of career. From what I've read about your expansion plans into new markets, there would be exciting opportunities to be part of something that is really scaling. I want to develop my skills in people management and commercial strategy, and I believe this role would be a strong foundation for exactly that."
💡 Useful phrases
"I'd like to highlight that..." — drawing attention to a key point
"In that situation, I decided to..." — beginning the Action part of STAR
"As a result, we were able to..." — opening the Result part of STAR
"I'm particularly proud of..." — emphasising an achievement
"That experience taught me..." — reflecting on a learning

Elevator Pitch
1 Write and practise your elevator pitch.
🛗 The Elevator Pitch Challenge

You have 60 seconds to sell yourself. Imagine you step into a lift with the CEO of your dream company. They glance over and ask: "So, what do you do?"

Write and practise your answer. Cover all four elements:

  • Who you are — your name and professional identity
  • What you do — your key skills or area of expertise
  • What makes you different — your unique value or achievement
  • What you are looking for — a natural, confident closing line
Sample pitch:

"Hi, I'm Ana — I'm a marketing strategist with eight years of experience helping B2B tech companies grow their presence in competitive markets. What makes my approach different is that I combine data analytics with creative storytelling, which helped my last company increase qualified leads by 60% in under a year. I'm actually exploring new opportunities in the SaaS space right now — I'd love to hear more about what your team is working on."

Notice: confident but not arrogant, specific (60% increase), and ends with a question to keep the conversation going.
